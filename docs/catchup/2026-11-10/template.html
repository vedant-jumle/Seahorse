The short version

The mechanism we built still works: a stored slice of the model's own keys and values (K,V), restored in a later session, brings a fact back, and the whole stored turn is exactly the same as the turn given as text. What changed in the last three weeks is the picture around it. We moved from hand-written facts to real memory benchmarks and compared our selectors and our memory format against published methods. The comparison mostly went against us as a system: published KV compression (KVzip) picks positions as well as or better than our best selector, and storing cache instead of text buys no accuracy at much higher storage. It went for us as understanding: the gap between "the content is there" and "the model is willing to use it" (the licence) shows up for every per-head selector we tested, and one method nearly closes it, which gives us a concrete thing to explain. On 7 Nov we split the work into a training-free understanding branch (this one) and a trained-parts branch.

The thread through the runs

Four runs, one question carried forward: when a memory holds the answer, what makes the model use it?

Background: the pieces you need for the rest

Seahorse is a training-free memory for a frozen LLM. It has had two channels. The steer channel adds a stored vector to the residual stream; paper 1 showed it carries a push (a leaning, a word to say), not a statement. The slice channel keeps the K,V the model itself computed for a few positions of an earlier turn and puts them back, at their stored positions, before a later question. Everything below is about slices.

Four ideas the results depend on

Teacher-forced vs judged generation

Teacher-forced (TF) scoring feeds the gold answer and reads how likely the model finds it: log P(answer). It is cheap and measures whether the content is reachable. Generation lets the model write its own answer, which three judges (Qwen3.5-9B, gpt-oss, Gemma-4) label against the benchmark's official prompt; the majority wins. It measures whether the model actually uses the memory.

Gap closed

One scale for every condition: (condition − no memory) / (whole turn − no memory). 0 = no better than no memory, 1 = as good as restoring the whole turn. It is computed both ways: on the TF answer score, and on judged correctness.

The licence

A slice can hold the value and the model can still refuse ("I don't have access to your personal information"). We call the missing piece the licence to answer. In dream_bind it was first read as a missing "told" signal, then (addendum 8) mostly a generic "answer personal questions" direction. The licence gap is the TF gap closed minus the judged gap closed for the same items: content looks present, use does not follow.

Storage order and binding

Two facts stored apart and restored together swap partners (wrong person's name 0.19–0.37 of the time), because every separately stored slice was encoded as "the first pair". Storing the k-th memory as the k-th fact fixes most of it at N = 3 and halves it at N = 5 (addenda 8–9). The binding sits at the subject's positions and is written only when facts are encoded together.

The core: dream_compare

dream_compare is the run that decided where the project stands. Stage A compares 18 ways of choosing which positions to keep, on one stored turn per question, against the whole turn. Stage B puts whole conversation histories into memory and compares text, full cache, our sparse slices and MemArt-style blocks on four benchmarks. Both are read against the licence story from dream_subj and dream_select (see the thread above).

Stage A (9 Nov): our selectors against the published ones

Context: dream_select showed that a slice can hold the answer and still not be used. Stage A asks whether that is our selectors' problem or every selector's.

Question
H1: does the real KVzip beat our S-recon? H2: do per-head positions beat one shared set? H3: is the licence gap general? H4: how does S-loo compare with the best baseline?
Setup
133 LongMemEval oracle items whose evidence is one user turn (106 primary). Budgets matched in stored K,V entries. Rules at k = 8, generation, 3-judge majority. Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.2-3B, Granite-4.2-30B (Gemma missing).
Result
KVzip-g (KVzip's official global budget over layers × heads) is the best selector on 7B, Llama and Granite. H1 holds on 3 of 4, H2 on 2 of 4, H3 on 4 of 4. S-loo is indistinguishable from the best baseline on the 1.5B and 7B and worse on Llama (−0.17) and Granite (−0.24).
Why might a global budget matter? (literature, not tested here) Three papers name non-uniform per-head budgets as the biggest single factor in KV compaction. Attention Matching's leave-one-out ablation (Zweiger et al. 2026, Fig. 5) ranks non-uniform head budgets as the most important component and self-study queries as the least. The Gating Mechanism paper (Gu et al., arXiv 2611.07605) moves RULER from 44.86 with a fixed shared gate mass and 58.86 with KL to the dense model to 67.60 with per-head budgets set by retained attention mass (dense 70.01). KVzip itself keeps the top r% of entries across all heads. KVzip-g is our only condition with a non-uniform budget over layers × heads, so "the licence lives in a few layers or heads that a uniform budget starves" is a candidate explanation. A caution: in Compression-Aware Abstention a real KVzip-compressed cache let Qwen2.5-7B answer only 4 of 97 items whose evidence survived, so KVzip is not a licence guarantee in every setting.
Means
As a selector, ours is not the contribution any more. The useful new fact is that one method keeps whatever carries the licence on the larger models. What it keeps is the open question that ties Stage A back to dream_subj.
Technical detail

H3 per model: d = gap_tf − gap_gen per item, for KVzip-ph, SnapKV-dream-ph, H2O-ph, VATP-ph; a model passes if ≥ 3 of 4 have a lower bound > 0. 1.5B passes 3 of 4 (H2O-ph fails); the others 4 of 4. KVzip-g was not one of the H3 methods. Paired S-loo − gold@k: better only on the 7B at k = 8 (+0.15 [0.03, 0.27]); indistinguishable at k = 4 and on every other model.

KVzip here is a faithful reimplementation in fp32 (the official code needs flash-attn / fp16); the repeat request is a new user turn. Expected Attention follows KVPress without AdaKV. Summary results/dream_compare_a_20261105_1822/summary_a.md; per model report_a.md, verdict_a.json. 26,300 judged answers per model.

Stage B (8–10 Nov, partial): memory paradigms end to end

Context: Stage A measured one stored turn at a time. Stage B asks whether any of it matters when the whole history is the memory and retrieval decides what comes back. Partial: Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B are analysed. Granite is still generating; Gemma-4 never ran. The cross-model summary has not run.

Question
With retrieval held fixed, does restoring cache (P3) beat inserting the same turns as text (P2)? What do sparse slices (P4) lose against full cache, and how do they compare with MemArt-style blocks at the same budget?
Setup
Whole histories as memory; every session encoded once. BM25 top-m turns (m = 3, 10; PersonaMem m = 1, 3 sessions). Benchmarks: LongMemEval oracle (500) and S-120, LoCoMo (1,986), PersonaMem-32k (589, multiple choice), BEAM-100K (320). One greedy answer per item; 3 judges.
Result
B1: text RAG beats KV RAG (pooled m = 3: 1.5B −0.025, 7B −0.040, Llama equivalent; m = 10: −0.039 to −0.048 on all three). B2: P4 loses 0.10–0.29 on LongMemEval and LoCoMo for 7–58× less storage than P3. B3: P4 beats MemArt-style blocks at the same budget on most cells. B5: P4 refuses more than P3 (the licence gap, end to end).
B3 and B5 per model and benchmark (m = 3)
Means
As a memory system, a stored cache does not pay on these benchmarks: MemArt's reviewers were right about storage. The P3 < P2 gap is itself an understanding result: a turn's cache carries its original context's attention, and restoring it without its neighbours costs a little, the same family of effect as storage-order collisions and CacheBlend's missing cross-attention.
Technical detail

B1 is a TOST: "equivalent" if the 90% CI of the paired difference lies inside ±0.03. Storage = (head + all turn tokens) × K,V numbers × 4 bytes for P3; P4 = 8 positions per turn plus the text index. LoCoMo's "refusal" counts "not mentioned" and is high for every condition. Per model results/dream_compare_b_20261105_1915/<model>/report_b.md, verdict_b.json (fetched 10 Nov); jobs.txt has every submission.

22–30 Oct · dream_bind addenda 7–9 (context)

Question
Why does an attention-only slice refuse, does the hybrid's state recognise the specific item, and does storage order cause the partner swaps?
Why
Paper 2 ("what binds a memory?") needed these before it could be written.
Result
  • The 2B's state does item recognition (rejects look-alike names), but a joint state of two facts does not bind (says yes to both names for either person).
  • The refusal fix ("told" direction) is mostly a generic comply direction: 2B 0.67 with the generic one vs 0.76 with told.
  • Storage order is the collision: storing the new fact second cuts partner confusion 0.33–0.41 → 0.09–0.15 on all three models. N = 3: 0.38–0.47 → 0.04–0.09. N = 5: only down to 0.22–0.30 (fails every rule).
  • Llama-3.2-3B replicates slice > text (1.00 vs 0.24) and the collision.
Means
The collision with a mechanism became paper 2's headline; the refusal became a secondary, mostly generic finding. That refusal is the "licence" that reappears in everything below.

22 Oct · dream_subj: the subject's position supplies the licence

Question
When a slice is restored, does answering a direct question need the subject's own position ("dog" in "My dog's name is Pepper."), or does any extra position help?
Why
R-min (name + full stop) reached 0.86 clean use on use prompts but only 0.66 on direct questions. Entity-tracking work predicts a two-step lookup: find the subject, then follow its tag to the attribute.
Setup
Qwen2.5-1.5B, local, teacher-forced only, 45 person and pet facts. Slices differ only in which positions are kept. Main measure: log P of the answer prefix ("Your dog's name is"), i.e. answering instead of refusing.
Result
Only the subject helps: +1.31 nats [1.01, 1.63] over R-min; "is" and "name" add nothing (+0.07, −0.21). Three positions get 81% of the way to the whole turn. Refusal mass 0.15 → 0.02; TF yes/no 0.82 → 1.00. The value's own probability is the same with every slice.
Means
R-min's weakness on direct questions was a missing subject position, not a missing value. The subject changes the decision to answer. It fits addendum 8's "binding at the subject's positions". It also tells us what a good selector must keep: not just the value.
Technical detail

Pre-registered verdict "subject position matters (two-step lookup)": all three of R+subj − R-min / − R+fill / − R+rel have lower bounds > 0. R-subj2 (subject + name, still 2 positions) also beats R-min (+0.51 [0.19, 0.84]). Attention: in layers 3–13 the question's "dog" attends to the restored "dog" (0.07–0.17 of its attention); in layers 23–27 the answer position attends to the name.

Limits: TF only, no judges; the 7 hand-written facts alone are not significant on the answer score (+0.46 [−0.20, 1.13]; three questions don't fit the prefix). It cannot separate a binding-ID lookup from "this subject was mentioned". Open discriminator: a "dog" entry from an unrelated sentence plus the Pepper slice.

4 Nov · dream_select: can the positions be chosen automatically?

Question
On real conversation turns, can the model choose which few positions to keep, without seeing the later question?
Why
Every slice so far was hand-picked. A memory system has to choose by itself, offline (the "dreaming" phase). This was the automatic version of the project.
Setup
LongMemEval oracle, 64 single-session-user items; the turn holding the answer is given (positions only, no retrieval). Selectors: S-dream (attention from 3 questions the model dreams about the turn; primary), S-attn, S-recon, S-loo (leave-one-out on the model's own answers to its dreams), random, last-k, gold@k. Local TF on Qwen2.5-1.5B first; then DelftBlue: TF + generation + 3 judges on Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.2-3B.
Result
S-dream beats random: TF on all 3 models (lower bounds 0.35 / 1.49 / 2.33 nats), judged on 7B (+0.08) and Llama (+0.12), not the 1.5B (+0.03): replicates, 2 of 3. But the primary rule (gap closed ≥ 0.5 and beating S-attn) fails everywhere, and absolute use is low. The attention selectors mostly keep punctuation (0.48 of S-dream's picks on the 1.5B; base rate 0.09).
Means
Our cluster threshold (S-dream clearly beats random) was met, so the larger comparison went ahead. The bigger lesson: a TF score is not evidence that a slice will be used. The answer's own tokens are not an oracle for generation.

Cluster: results/dream_select_cluster_20261104_0129/summary.md, <model>/report_gen.md, <model>/tf/report.md. 5,320 answers per model, 1 greedy + 4 samples, ≤ 64 tokens; judge agreement 0.96. 7 Nov · SLEEP check, Stage B repair, and the fork

SLEEP
Google's SLEEP (Behrouz et al., arXiv 2606.03979) is weight-based continual learning: new low-rank experts, distillation + RL, LoRA "dreaming" on self-generated data. Not frozen, no KV or activation memory, so no collision with our work. The word "dreaming" and offline consolidation as an idea are taken. Still ours: training-free, between-session consolidation on a frozen model's own KV positions and recurrent state (selection, storage order, joint re-encoding). Avoid "self-learning memory" outward; say "a memory the frozen model maintains itself".
Repair
Three Stage B jobs had run out of GPU memory (Llama PersonaMem and BEAM, Granite BEAM). Batch budgets were fixed (infrastructure only) and the jobs resubmitted with continuations. Llama then finished; Granite BEAM kept failing.
Fork
The conversation was split. This branch: training-free, understanding first (what a KV memory needs, why it fails). The sibling branch: small trained parts on a frozen backbone: an amortised S-loo, a licence gate or adapter, a learned key with verification, slice → LoRA consolidation, a trained binding signal. Its literature synthesis is docs/trained/README.md.
Sources

docs/dreaming/literature-sleep.md (4066774; 21 PDFs in papers/sleep-consolidation/); docs/trained/README.md (c888943). PREREG Stage B "Repair, 2026-11-07". What it means looking ahead

Claims that survive

  • A 2-position slice carries a fact across sessions and beats the same tokens as text (Qwen2.5, the 2B, Llama).
  • The answer reads the value late from its own position; the subject's position decides whether the model answers (dream_subj).
  • Separately stored slices swap partners by storage order; storing in order fixes N = 3 and halves N = 5.
  • On the hybrid, the recurrent state is recognition and the attention entries are recall.
  • Teacher-forced scores overstate generation for every per-head selector (H3, 4 of 4 models), and the failure is mostly refusal.
  • The whole turn restored = the turn as text (to ~1e-4), on every model and benchmark checked.

Claims retracted or weakened

  • "S-loo is the best automatic selector we tested." KVzip-g matches it on the 7B and beats it on Llama and Granite.
  • "S-loo beats the answer span (gold@k)." Paired, only on the 7B at k = 8 (+0.15); nowhere else.
  • "The licence gap is a property of slices." KVzip-g nearly closes it on the 7B and Llama, so it depends on what is kept. (It remains large on the 1.5B.)
  • "Stored cache keeps more than text." True only for tiny slices against their own tokens; at the turn level text RAG is as good or better (B1).
  • Dreamed questions as a new selector; cross-session KV reuse as new; "self-learning memory" as framing.

Open questions

  • What does KVzip-g keep that carries the licence? Its global budget lets some layers and heads keep many positions and others none. Does it keep the subject span, the entities, or specific early layers? dream_subj predicts the subject.
  • Is the licence a matter of attention mass? Attention Matching's β (a per-entry bias so a compact cache keeps the original's mass) is an untrained test; the trained branch lists it as the first thing to try.
  • Why does the 1.5B behave differently? KVzip-g's TF and judged gap closed are 0.59 vs 0.21 there; the 1.5B also refuses far more with no memory (0.85).
  • Is P3 < P2 the collision effect at scale? A turn encoded inside its session and restored alone lacks its neighbours. Would storing turns in order, or re-encoding the retrieved set jointly, close it?
  • Does cache memory pay anywhere? Only time to first token favours it so far. Long histories with many questions per conversation, or the hybrid's fixed-size state, are the places it could.

Glossary

Slices and conditions

slice
The cached K,V of some positions of a stored turn, computed in its original context, restored at their stored RoPE positions.
nomem
No memory: the question alone.
ctx
The experience sentence in the same user turn as the question.
R-full
Every position of the stored turn restored. Equals T-full (the turn as text in an earlier turn).
R-content
R-full without the turn's template tokens.
R-min
2 positions: the value and the next token.
R+subj, R+fill, R+rel
R-min plus the subject's last token / the "is" before the value / the token "name".
R-subj2
Subject + value (2 positions).
R-orphan
A slice harvested after an unrelated exchange.
T-min, T-subj3
The same tokens as R-min / R+subj given as plain text.
R-min-state
On the hybrid 2B: 2 attention positions plus the DeltaNet recurrent state.
gold
The answer's tokens + 1 (uses the label; verbatim items only).
gold@k
k tokens around the answer (uses the label).
k, k10
Positions kept per memory; k10 = 10% of the turn's content tokens (min 2). In Stage A, matched in K,V entries.

Selectors

S-dream
Attention to the turn from 3 questions the model dreams about it (shared positions).
S-attn
Mean attention a position receives inside the turn (H2O family).
S-recon
Attention while the model repeats the turn (a mean-pooled, shared form of KVzip's idea).
S-loo
Leave-one-out: drop each position, measure the drop in log P of the model's own answers to its dreamed questions.
S-dream-VATP
S-dream's attention × the value norm (exploratory).
random, last-k
Random positions (seeded) / the last k tokens.
KVzip-g / -ph / -sh
KVzip (Kim et al. 2025): max attention during a "repeat the context" pass. g = one global budget over all layers and KV heads (official); ph = top-k per head; sh = one shared set.
SnapKV-dream / -oracle
SnapKV's pooled observation-window vote, with the dreamed questions / the real question (a cheating ceiling).
H2O
Accumulated attention (heavy hitters) + recent tokens.
VATP
H2O's score × the value's l1 norm.
EA (Expected Attention)
Attention from a Gaussian model of future queries, × value norm (KVPress).
StreamingLLM
The first and last tokens (sink + recent window).
per-head (ph) vs shared (sh)
Each (layer, KV head) keeps its own positions vs one set for all.

Paradigms and rules

P0 … P5s
Stage B memory paradigms: no memory, full context, text RAG, KV RAG, Seahorse sparse (P4loo / P4dream), MemArt-style blocks (P5; P5s matched to P4's budget). See the table in Stage B.
m
Turns retrieved by BM25 (3 or 10; PersonaMem sessions, 1 or 3).
H1–H4
Stage A rules: KVzip > S-recon; per-head > shared; the licence gap is general; S-loo vs the best baseline.
B1–B5
Stage B rules: text vs KV equivalence; sparse vs full; sparse vs MemArt at equal storage; Pareto frontier; refusal per paradigm.
gap closed
(condition − nomem) / (R-full − nomem), on the TF answer score or on judged correctness.
licence
What makes the model answer from a memory rather than refuse; the licence gap = TF gap closed − judged gap closed.
storage order
The order in which memories are encoded; storing the k-th memory as the k-th fact gives each its own binding tag.
judges
Qwen3.5-9B (original), gpt-oss, Gemma-4; majority, ties to the original.
